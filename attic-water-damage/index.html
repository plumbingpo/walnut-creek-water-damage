Attic Water Damage: What Walnut Creek Homeowners Should Know

There are two ways an attic ends up wet. Rain gets in from above through worn shingles, failed flashing, or the seals around vents and chimneys. From below, household moisture rises and condenses on the cold roof sheathing if attic ventilation is poor. Whichever it is, few people ever go up and look. For Walnut Creek homeowners, our crew examines the attic, identifies where the water comes from, and dries the space.

Most attic jobs center on wet insulation. Fiberglass and cellulose both lose R-value when they get wet, so the home costs more to heat and cool. Even worse, it keeps water pressed against the framing and the ceiling drywall for weeks. Saturated material comes out, air movers and dehumidifiers dry the wood, and any mold on the sheathing or rafters gets treated.

Signs to Look For

  • Nail points coming through the sheathing that are rusty or beaded with water
  • A musty smell in the attic or in the upstairs closets
  • Daylight showing around a vent pipe, chimney, or along a roof valley
  • Bubbling or peeling ceiling paint in a room on the top floor
  • A patch of insulation that is flatter, more matted, or darker than the rest
  • A bath fan or ceiling light fixture with drips or a stain around it
What You Cannot See

What Hides Behind It

Misrouted Exhaust Fans

Every day, a fan duct that stops short of the roof releases humid bathroom air into the attic. The condensation it leaves on the sheathing can pass for a roof leak.

Ceiling Collapse

Insulation full of water weighs a lot. Drywall that has been wet from above weakens at the fasteners and can drop suddenly, bringing wet insulation down with it.

Insulation That Stops Working

Insulation that is damp and compressed barely insulates, and drying may not bring it back. Rooms under the wet section run hotter or colder, and energy bills rise.

Mold Under the Roof Deck

Poorly ventilated attics often grow mold across the underside of the sheathing. It can cover a wide area before anyone goes up to look.

Our Attic Water Damage Process

1

Inspect and Find the Source

We check the roof deck, flashing points, vents and exhaust ducts to learn whether the water is a leak, condensation, or both. How far it spread is shown by moisture meters and thermal imaging.

2

Take Out Wet Insulation

We bag and remove insulation that is moldy or saturated, which lets the drywall and wood beneath it dry. We protect the living space below and keep the access path contained.

3

Dry the Wood

With dehumidifiers and air movers, the sheathing, joists and rafters return to normal moisture levels. An antimicrobial goes on after any visible mold has been cleaned from the wood.

You are shown the ventilation or roof problem to be corrected, and our findings can go to your roofer. When the attic has stayed dry, the R-value is restored with new insulation.

The Clock

How the Damage Progresses

First storm

Insulation Soaks It Up

Rain that makes it through the roof falls into the insulation and is absorbed. Nothing shows indoors, so the leak goes unnoticed and the roof is not repaired.

Hours 24 to 48

Mold Can Start

Mold does very well on wet paper facing and damp lumber when the attic is warm and the air is still. It can start on the back side of the ceiling drywall and on the sheathing.

Weeks later

Stains Come Through

Once storm after storm has saturated the insulation, the ceiling starts getting wet. Brown rings appear in the room below, and the drywall may start to sag.

Months later

Wood Starts Rotting

Sheathing delaminates and loses its hold on the roofing nails. Mold spreads across large sections of the roof deck, and the rafters remain damp enough to decay.
