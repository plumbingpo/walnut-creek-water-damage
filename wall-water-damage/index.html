Wall and Drywall Water Damage: What Walnut Creek Homeowners Should Know

A sheet of drywall behaves much as a wick does. Once the base of a wall gets wet, moisture climbs through the paper and gypsum, commonly a foot or more higher than the water stood on the floor. From the room, the surface may seem dry and sound even though the hidden face and the insulation are saturated. That is why we check walls with a moisture meter instead of trusting how they look.

A wet wall does not automatically mean demolition. When the water was clean and the drywall remains firm, drying it where it stands is often possible, at times by pulling the baseboard and pushing air into the cavity through small holes. If the drywall has gone soft, crumbles, shows mold or took in dirty water, we do a flood cut, meaning a level cut over the damaged area with all material beneath it removed.

Warning Signs

  • Door casing and trim joints separating from the drywall
  • Discolored lower drywall with a visible line marking how high water wicked
  • Bubbling, wrinkled or peeling paint and wallpaper low on the wall
  • Fuzzy or dark patches hidden in corners or in back of furniture
  • A wall that feels cool to the touch compared with others
  • A wall surface that gives, feels mushy or breaks apart under your thumb
Save or Replace

What Stays and What Goes

It depends on the material, how long it stayed wet and how clean the water was. We test before we decide, and we tell you why.

How We Fix It

Our Wall and Drywall Water Damage Process

1

Map the Moisture

We scan each wall with a moisture meter and thermal camera and mark how high and how far the water traveled, including the opposite side and adjoining rooms.

2

Decide What Stays

You get a straight answer on what can be dried where it is and what must be removed, judged by the category of water, the time it sat and the firmness of the drywall.

3

Cut or Ventilate

Where the drywall stays, the trim is pulled and airflow is sent inside the wall. For walls being removed, we make a level flood cut and take out wet insulation.

4

Drying and Reconstruction

Drying typically takes 3 to 5 days of air movement and dehumidification before the framing and surviving drywall meter dry. After that comes insulation, new drywall hung and finished, trim put back and paint.

Why Speed Matters

The Damage Timeline

The first few hours

Wicking Begins

Moisture enters where the panel meets the floor and wicks upward from there. The trim and the framing plate at floor level get wet too.

24 to 48 hours

Wall Swells

The wet line rises higher, baseboards swell and paint begins to blister. Inside the cavity, the paper face becomes a place mold can begin.

Days 3 through 7

Panels Lose Strength

Gypsum softens and the paper lets go. A musty odor grows stronger while the batts settle and mold covers more of the unseen face.

After a week

Framing Is Affected

Studs and plates stay wet long enough to grow mold and begin to decay. Much more wall now has to be removed and rebuilt.

Behind the Surface

What Hides Behind It

Insulation Holding Water

Once wet, fiberglass insulation stays wet and keeps the studs damp. Drying from inside the room is not enough when the insulation is soaked.

Hidden Behind Cabinets

Wet drywall behind cabinets, vanities and built-ins gets no airflow and is easy to overlook. Checking under the cabinet base and on the reverse face tells us if it is wet.

Mold Inside Cavities

Paper lining, darkness and stagnant air make the hidden side of a wall a perfect place for mold. That side may be coated in growth even as the room side appears spotless.

Wet Sheathing Outside

Outside walls have sheathing past the insulation that can also get soaked. It gets a moisture reading prior to closing, since it holds dampness a long time.
